To the editors of \textit{Computational Linguistics}:

We have enclosed our revised manuscript entitled \textit{A Stylometric Application of Large Language Models}. We appreciate the thoughtful and constructive feedback from the editor, action editor, and reviewers.

The primary concerns raised by the reviewers centered on (a) the relationship between our approach and concurrent work by Huang et al.\ (2025; referred to as Huang et al.\ 2024 in the reviewer comments, now published in \textit{PLoS ONE}), (b) the lack of evaluation on standard authorship attribution benchmarks, and (c) the suggestion to use more modern or larger language models. We have substantially revised the manuscript to address these concerns, including two new analyses:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A systematic dataset-size analysis characterizing the relationship between training corpus size and attribution accuracy (1,520 newly trained models), including a formal sigmoid fit estimating that $\geq 95\%$ expected accuracy requires approximately 51,000 tokens per author (new Section ``Training data requirements,'' \pref{XX}).
\item A comparison with pre-trained text embedding models from the MTEB leaderboard, demonstrating that even state-of-the-art embedding models (up to 4 billion parameters) \colorbox{yellow}{do not match} the 100\% accuracy achieved by our approach using much smaller (0.8M parameter) author-specific models (new Section ``Comparison with text embedding methods,'' \pref{XX}).
\end{enumerate}

We have carefully addressed each of the comments from the reviewers, and we provide detailed point-by-point responses on the following pages. The reviewers' comments are \textit{italicized} and our responses are in \textbf{bold}.

Thank you for considering our revised manuscript.

Sincerely,

Jeremy R. Manning

\textbf{\large Response to the Action Editor}

\textit{This submission proposes to utilize large language models as kind of stylometric measure for applications such as author attribution. For each studied author, this method will train a GPT-2 model from scratch using books of the author, and then uses cross-entropy loss on held-out texts as a proxy for stylistic similarity. The underlying assumption is that the model trained on author A's books should assign lower loss to A's text than others'. This is a simple but effective method, which is supported by experiments on 8 English-language writers during 19th--20th century, with 100\% attribution accuracy.}

\textit{As indicated by the reviewers, the proposed method is very close to the Authorial Language Models (ALMs, Huang et al., 2024), which fine-tune GPT-2 per author and attribute authorship via perplexity. This makes the main contribution of this submission not as novel as expected, in terms of methodology and associated analysis. Although the authors briefly discussed Huang et al.\ (2024) in section 4.1, there is no explicit discussion about why the proposed method/study is novel given existing works.}

\textbf{We have substantially expanded our discussion of the relationship to Huang et al.\ (2025) in the revised Section ``Relationship to prior work'' (\pref{XX}). We now explicitly articulate the key methodological distinction---training from scratch versus fine-tuning---and its implications for data purity, as well as several additional contributions unique to our work. We discuss this in detail in our responses to Reviewers 1 and 3 below.}

\textit{If the authors want to establish their contributions in the ground of using LLMs to analyze authorship attributions, then it would be necessary to tell a different story. Possible analyses would include:}

\textit{$\bullet$ how much data one should have at least to train the GPT-2 model?}

\textbf{We have added a new dataset-size analysis (Section ``Training data requirements,'' \pref{XX}) in which we systematically varied the number of training tokens per author from 2,500 to 643,041. This required training 1,520 new models (8 authors $\times$ 10 seeds $\times$ 19 token levels). We fit a four-parameter sigmoid to the resulting accuracy curve ($R^2 = 0.979$) and estimated that $\geq 95\%$ expected accuracy requires approximately 51,000 tokens per author (bootstrap 95\% CI: 12,000--70,000 tokens). The new figure shows per-author accuracy as colored dots and the fitted sigmoid with a 95\% confidence interval ribbon:}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{../figs/source/accuracy_vs_tokens_sigmoid.pdf}
\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{../figs/source/t_test_ntokens.pdf}
\end{center}

\textbf{Panel A shows that attribution accuracy follows a sigmoidal trajectory on the $\log_{10}$-token scale, with some authors (e.g., Austen, Baum) reliably attributed even at low token counts, while others (e.g., Melville, Twain) require substantially more data. Panel B shows that the corresponding $t$-statistics (comparing same-author versus other-author losses at the final training epoch) increase monotonically with training corpus size for all authors.}

\textit{$\bullet$ comprehensive evaluation over existing benchmarks with competitive methods}

\textbf{We respectfully note that the data requirements of our approach make standard authorship attribution benchmarks infeasible for models trained from scratch. The benchmarks used by Huang et al.\ (2025) provide far fewer tokens per author than our method requires:}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Benchmark} & \textbf{Avg.\ tokens/text} & \textbf{Our threshold} \\
\midrule
Blogs50 & 112 & \\
CCAT50 & 506 & $\sim$51,000 \\
Guardian & 1,052 & (bootstrap 95\% CI: \\
IMDB62 & 349 & 12,000--70,000) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{This reflects a fundamental difference between fine-tuning pre-trained models (which inherit general language representations and thus require less author-specific data) and training from scratch (which must learn all patterns from the author's text alone). Rather than force our method into an unsuitable evaluation regime, we have instead added a comparison with pre-trained text embedding models (see below and new Section ``Comparison with text embedding methods,'' \pref{XX}).}

\textit{$\bullet$ what will happen if different comparing authors have different amount of books available for training?}

\textbf{Our new dataset-size analysis directly addresses this. As visible in the figure above (Panel A), we found substantial variability across authors. Some authors (e.g., Austen, Baum) were reliably attributed even with 2,500 tokens, while others (e.g., Melville, Twain) required considerably more data. From the revised manuscript (\pref{XX}):}

\textbf{\begin{quote}
``Individual authors showed substantial variability: some authors (e.g., Austen, Baum) were reliably attributed even at low token counts, while others (e.g., Melville, Twain) required considerably more data.''
\end{quote}}

\textit{$\bullet$ what will happen if authors have quite different writing styles, genres, occasions (formal writing v.s.\ on X) during their careers? or, with quite different writing topics.}

\textbf{Our existing Oz book analysis provides some evidence of cross-topic robustness: models trained on an author's general works correctly attribute a contested text from a different series. We have expanded our discussion of this limitation (\pref{XX}) and identified systematic cross-domain evaluation as an important direction for future work. However, constructing new multi-genre corpora for the same authors is beyond the scope of the current study.}

Additionally, we have added a comparison with pre-trained text embedding models. Using three models from the MTEB leaderboard spanning 137M to 4.0B parameters, embedding-based nearest-neighbor classification achieved at most \colorbox{yellow}{81\%} book-level accuracy on our 84-book corpus---compared to 100\% for our approach using models with just 0.8M parameters each. \colorbox{yellow}{This demonstrates that author-specific language models capture stylistic patterns that generic text embeddings do not.}

\textbf{\large Reviewer 1}

\textit{Recommendation: Decline Submission}

\textit{2. What is this paper about?}

\textit{The paper proposes ``predictive comparison'' as an LLM-based stylometric measure. For each author, the paper trains a small GPT-2 model from scratch on that author's books, then uses cross-entropy loss on held-out text as a proxy for stylistic similarity: a model should assign lower loss to its own author's text than to others'.}

\textit{They demonstrate: On eight 19th--20th century English-language authors, each author-specific model assigns the lowest loss to its own held-out book, giving 100\% attribution accuracy in this closed set. The cross-entropy matrix over (model, test-author) is used to define a stylometric distance and visualized via MDS to explore relationships between authors.}

\textit{Conceptually, this is very close to Huang et al.'s Authorial Language Models (ALMs), which fine-tune GPT-2 per author and attribute authorship via perplexity on benchmark datasets (Blogs50, CCAT50, Guardian, IMDB62), also analyzing token-level contributions and the relative informativeness of content vs function words.}

\textit{3. Strengths:}

\textit{Clear and intuitive core idea. Training a separate LM per author and using cross-entropy as a relative stylometric measure is conceptually simple and easy to understand.}

\textit{Strong case studies. Perfect classification of held-out books across eight authors is impressive and shows that even relatively small GPT-2 models can encode robust author-specific patterns.}

\textit{The Oz-book experiment nicely connects the method to a classical attribution problem, replicating prior stylometric conclusions in an LLM framework and demonstrating robustness across within-author topical variation.}

\textit{Thoughtful ablations. The content-only, function-only, and POS-only conditions give insight into what the models are actually using, contributing to the broader debate about content vs function words in authorship attribution.}

\textbf{We thank the reviewer for their positive assessment of the core idea, case studies, and ablation analyses!}

\textit{4. Weaknesses:}

\textit{Incremental novelty given existing publication Huang et al.\ 2025. Methodologically, predictive comparison is extremely close to Huang et al.\ 2025: both train one causal LM per author (from-scratch vs fine-tuned) and attribute authorship via perplexity / cross-entropy on candidate texts.}

\textit{Moreover, it is hard to consider the paper as an independent work of Huang et al.\ 2025, because the latter has been posted on ArXiv in Feb.\ 2024 and published in July 2025.}

\textbf{A critical methodological distinction separates the two approaches: we train models entirely from scratch, whereas Huang et al.\ fine-tune a pre-trained GPT-2 model. Fine-tuning inherits the representations learned during pre-training on WebText (a 40~GB web corpus), meaning that ``held-out'' evaluation data may have been seen during pre-training. Training from scratch eliminates this data contamination risk entirely, because the only text the model has ever seen is the author's own corpus. This is particularly important for forensic applications where the provenance of model knowledge must be established. From the revised manuscript (\pref{XX}):}

\textbf{\begin{quote}
``A critical methodological distinction separates the two approaches. [Huang et al.] fine-tune a pre-trained GPT-2 model for each author, whereas we train models entirely from scratch. Fine-tuning inherits the representations learned during pre-training on WebText, a 40 GB corpus of web text. Although [Huang et al.] argue that their training data is unlikely to appear in WebText, this cannot be guaranteed for all evaluation corpora---a concern that extends to any approach based on fine-tuning pre-trained models. Training from scratch eliminates this data contamination risk entirely.''
\end{quote}}

\textbf{Regarding the timeline: our work was developed independently and concurrently with Huang et al. Both manuscripts were posted as preprints in early-to-mid 2024. We view this convergent development as evidence that predictive modeling may be a natural framework for computational stylometry.}

\textit{The paper does not argue for their main technical advances (training from scratch, one specific distance definition) given existing literature. No comparison to standard authorship benchmarks (Blogs50, CCAT50, Guardian, IMDB62) used in Huang et al.\ 2025.}

\textbf{Beyond the data purity distinction, our paper contributes several elements not present in Huang et al.\ (2025): (1) the stylometric distance measure $d(i,j)$ and its MDS visualization, (2) the application to the contested Oz authorship problem, (3) ablation studies decomposing the contributions of content words, function words, and parts of speech, (4) the new dataset-size analysis characterizing data requirements for training from scratch, and (5) the comparison with text embedding methods. We have made these contributions explicit in the revised Discussion (\pref{XX}).}

\textit{5. Substantive Revisions to be Required:}

\textit{Clarify the novelty claims relative to Huang et al.\ 2025 and prior information-theoretic stylometry.}

\textit{Explicitly explain what is genuinely new here beyond Huang et al.\ (2025): e.g., the focus on long-form literary texts, the definition and use of the stylometric distance d(i,j), the Oz case study, or methodological differences (from-scratch vs fine-tuning).}

\textbf{Done. We have expanded the ``Relationship to prior work'' section (\pref{XX}) as described above, explicitly articulating the five contributions that distinguish our work from Huang et al.\ (2025). We have also expanded our discussion of prior information-theoretic stylometry, including Juola \& Baayen (2005) and Zhao et al.\ (2006), clarifying how our approach extends this line of reasoning to large language models.}

\textit{Strengthen empirical evaluation. Add at least one experiment that goes beyond the eight-author, long-book setting---for example: A subset of a standard benchmark (e.g., Blogs50 or IMDB62) using predictive comparison, to show behavior on many authors and short texts.}

\textbf{As detailed in our response to the action editor above, the standard benchmarks are infeasible for our approach because they provide far fewer tokens per author ($\sim$100--1,000 tokens) than our method requires when training from scratch ($\sim$51,000 tokens). Rather than applying our method in a regime where it cannot be expected to succeed, we have (a) formally characterized the data requirements of our approach through the new dataset-size analysis, providing a principled explanation for why these benchmarks are unsuitable, and (b) added a comparison with text embedding methods that operate on the same data as our approach, providing empirical context for our results.}

\textbf{\large Reviewer 2}

\textit{Recommendation: Revisions Required (Accept with minor revisions: Only minor points to be addressed. Further Work Required.)}

\textit{2. What is this paper about?}

\textit{This paper introduced predictive comparison. It is a stylometric method which leverages LLMs to quantify the style by using predictive performance instead of / rather than explicit classification. The main idea is to train GPT-2 language model from scratch on each author's work, and use the cross-entropy loss on the held-out texts to measure the stylistic wise similarity. The proposed approach shows the power via the evaluation on the corpus, where the books from 8 great authors achieve the perfect attribution accuracy.}

\textit{3. Strengths:}

\textit{The paper figures out the authorship attribution problem / question via LLMs problem instead of / rather than using a classifier. The cross-entropy loss is intuitive to align with the stylometric signal. Further more, the proposed method achieves perfect attribution accuracy on the books from 8 authors.}

\textbf{We thank the reviewer for their positive assessment!}

\textit{4. Weaknesses:}

\textit{A key problem is the current study only relies on training on the small GPT-2 model from scratch. The work did NOT evaluate any recent / widely used open-source models (e.g., LLaMA, Qwen, DeepSeek). It also did NOT use any other training frames, such as continued pretraining or LoRA, instead of training from scratch.}

\textbf{We appreciate this concern. We address it in two parts:}

\textbf{First, if we fine-tune a pre-trained model---whether GPT-2, LLaMA, or any other---we are essentially adopting the same approach as Huang et al.\ (2025), with the same data contamination concerns described above. The methodological contribution of our work lies specifically in training from scratch, which guarantees that the only text the model has ever seen is the author's own corpus.}

\textbf{Second, our method already achieves 100\% attribution accuracy using a small GPT-2 architecture with just 0.8M non-embedding parameters. A more powerful model is unnecessary---the bottleneck is not model capacity but the amount of author-specific training data available. Our new dataset-size analysis (\pref{XX}) demonstrates this directly: accuracy is determined by training corpus size, not model size.}

\textit{6. Minor Revisions to be Required:}

\textit{I would suggest using the off-the-shelf widely used open-source models (e.g., LLaMA, Qwen, DeepSeek) to enhance the experiments (including the comparisons among different models). In addition, we can do some studies by using the continued pretraining or LoRA based on those SOTA open-source models instead of training from scratch.}

\textbf{Rather than fine-tuning larger pre-trained models (which would replicate Huang et al.'s approach), we have instead compared our method against pre-trained text embedding models. These models---including Qwen3-Embedding-4B (4.0 billion parameters)---represent the state of the art in general-purpose text representations. Despite being orders of magnitude larger than our author-specific models, even the best embedding model achieved only \colorbox{yellow}{81\%} book-level accuracy versus our 100\%. This suggests that the value of our approach lies not in model size but in the author-specific training objective.}

\textbf{We acknowledge that for more challenging settings (e.g., hundreds of authors with similar styles), larger models or parameter-efficient fine-tuning might offer benefits. We discuss this as a future direction in the revised manuscript (\pref{XX}).}

\textbf{\large Reviewer 3}

\textit{Recommendation: Revisions Required (Revise and resubmit for review: Needs more than minor changes. Further Work Required. Presentational Revisions Required.)}

\textit{2. What is this paper about?}

\textit{This paper introduces ``predictive comparison,'' a stylometric method that utilizes Large Language Models (LLMs) trained from scratch on specific authors to determine authorship attribution. The authors train GPT-2 models on the corpora of eight classic authors and measure the cross-entropy loss on held-out texts. The core premise is that a model trained on Author A will yield lower loss on unseen text by Author A than a model trained on Author B.}

\textit{3. Strengths:}

\textit{The authors implement controls including standardizing the token budget across authors and using bootstrapping with 10 random seeds to estimate confidence intervals. The breakdown of performance using Content-only, Function-only, and POS-only corpora provides insight.}

\textbf{We thank the reviewer for recognizing these methodological contributions.}

\textit{4. Weaknesses:}

\textit{There are several weaknesses that require further revisement:}

\textit{The novelty of this work: You mentioned Huang et al.'s work for using perplexity for authorship analysis, however, there are many existing works utilizing cross-entropy for authorship analysis. You should also compare it with those works.}

\textbf{We have expanded the ``Relationship to prior work'' section (\pref{XX}) to more thoroughly discuss prior work using cross-entropy and relative entropy for stylometry, including Juola \& Baayen (2005) and Zhao et al.\ (2006). From the revised manuscript:}

\textbf{\begin{quote}
``The information-theoretic foundations of our approach connect to earlier work using cross-entropy (Juola \& Baayen, 2005) and relative entropy (Zhao et al., 2006) for stylometry. These methods recognized that authorial style manifests not just in feature frequencies but in their sequential dependencies---precisely what language models are designed to capture. Our contribution extends this line of reasoning to large language models, which can learn these dependencies implicitly rather than requiring explicit feature engineering.''

\textbf{We have addressed this in two ways.
