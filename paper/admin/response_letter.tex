We thank the editor, action editor, and reviewers for their thoughtful and
constructive feedback. We have substantially revised the manuscript to address
the points raised, including two new analyses and expanded discussion. Below we
respond to each point in detail. Our responses are shown in \response{blue}.

\textbf{Note on citation:} Several reviews reference ``Huang et al.\ (2024).''
This paper has since been published as Huang et al.\ (2025) in \textit{PLoS
ONE} (doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0327081). We use the 2025 citation throughout.

\section*{Response to the Editor and Action Editor}

The action editor suggested several possible analyses, including: (a) how much
data is needed to train the model; (b) evaluation on existing benchmarks; (c)
the effect of varying training corpus sizes across authors; (d) robustness to
different genres/topics; and other directions.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
\item \textbf{How much data is needed?}

\response{We have added a new analysis systematically varying the number of
training tokens per author from 2,500 to 643,041 (Section~X, Figure~X). We
fit a four-parameter sigmoid to attribution accuracy as a function of
$\log_{10}$ training tokens ($R^2 = 0.979$) and estimated that $\geq 95\%$
expected accuracy requires approximately 51,000 tokens per author (bootstrap
95\% CI: 12,000--70,000 tokens). This analysis is based on 1,520 newly trained
models (8 authors $\times$ 10 seeds $\times$ 19 token levels).}

\item \textbf{Evaluation on existing benchmarks.}

\response{We respectfully note that the data requirements of our approach make
standard authorship attribution benchmarks infeasible. Our dataset-size
analysis shows that approximately 51,000 tokens per author are needed for
reliable attribution when training models from scratch. The benchmarks used by
Huang et al.\ (2025)---Blogs50 (avg.\ 112 tokens/text), CCAT50 (avg.\ 506
tokens/text), Guardian (avg.\ 1,052 tokens/text), and IMDB62 (avg.\ 349
tokens/text)---provide orders of magnitude less text per author than our method
requires. This is not a limitation of our method \textit{per se}, but rather a
consequence of the fundamental difference between fine-tuning pre-trained
models (which inherit general language representations) and training from
scratch (which must learn all patterns from the author's text alone).}

\item \textbf{Varying training corpus sizes across authors.}

\response{Our new dataset-size analysis addresses this directly. We found
substantial variability across authors: some (e.g., Austen, Baum) were
reliably attributed even with 2,500 tokens, while others (e.g., Melville,
Twain) required considerably more data. These differences are captured in
Figure~X.}

\item \textbf{Robustness to different genres/topics.}

\response{Our existing Oz book analysis provides some evidence of cross-topic
robustness (models trained on fantasy correctly attribute a contested text),
and we have expanded our discussion of this limitation. Systematic
cross-domain evaluation is an important direction for future work, but is
beyond the scope of the current study given that it would require collecting
and curating new multi-genre corpora for the same authors.}
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Response to Reviewer 1}

\textbf{R1 Main concern:} Incremental novelty relative to Huang et al.\ (2025).

\response{We have substantially expanded our discussion of the relationship
to Huang et al.\ (2025) and clarified the key methodological distinction:
we train models entirely from scratch, whereas Huang et al.\ fine-tune
pre-trained GPT-2. This distinction has important implications for data
purity. Fine-tuning inherits representations learned during pre-training on
WebText (a 40 GB web corpus), meaning that the ``held-out'' evaluation data
may have been seen during pre-training. Training from scratch eliminates this
contamination risk entirely, because the only text the model has ever seen is
the author's own corpus. This is particularly important for forensic
applications.}

\response{The two approaches also operate in fundamentally different data
regimes. Our dataset-size analysis shows that training from scratch requires
approximately 51,000 tokens per author for $\geq 95\%$ accuracy, whereas
fine-tuning leverages pre-trained representations and can work with much
shorter texts. We view these as complementary contributions: Huang et al.\
demonstrate that fine-tuning is effective for short-text benchmarks, while we
demonstrate that training from scratch---with its stronger data purity
guarantees---is effective for longer texts.}

\textbf{R1:} No comparison to standard authorship benchmarks (Blogs50, CCAT50,
Guardian, IMDB62).

\response{As detailed above, these benchmarks are infeasible for our approach
due to their short text lengths (112--1,052 tokens per text vs.\ our
requirement of $\sim$51,000 tokens per author). Rather than force our method
into an unsuitable evaluation regime, we have instead: (a) formally
characterized the data requirements of our approach (Figure~X), and (b) added
a comparison with text embedding methods that can operate on the same data as
our approach.}

\section*{Response to Reviewer 2}

\textbf{R2:} Should evaluate using more modern models (LLaMA, Qwen, DeepSeek)
or use continued pretraining/LoRA.

\response{We respectfully push back on this suggestion for two reasons.
First, if we fine-tune a pre-trained model (whether GPT-2, LLaMA, or any
other), we are essentially adopting the same approach as Huang et al.\ (2025),
with the same data contamination concerns described above. The value of our
approach lies specifically in training from scratch.

Second, our method already achieves 100\% attribution accuracy on our
8-author corpus using a small GPT-2 architecture (0.8M non-embedding
parameters). If the goal is reliable authorship attribution, a more powerful
model is unnecessary---the bottleneck is not model capacity but the amount of
author-specific training data available (as demonstrated by our dataset-size
analysis). Using a larger model would increase computational cost without
improving attribution accuracy for our evaluation setting.

That said, we acknowledge that for more challenging settings (e.g., hundreds of
authors with similar styles), larger models might offer benefits, and we
mention this as a future direction.}

\section*{Response to Reviewer 3}

\textbf{R3 Main concern:} Novelty relative to existing work, and lack of
benchmarks and baselines.

\response{We have addressed the novelty concern through expanded discussion
of the differences from Huang et al.\ (2025) (see our response to R1 above).
Regarding benchmarks, our new dataset-size analysis provides a principled
explanation for why standard short-text benchmarks are not applicable to our
from-scratch training approach.}

\textbf{R3:} Limited scope (8 authors from Project Gutenberg).

\response{We agree that the current scope is limited, and we have been
transparent about this as a limitation. However, we note that (a) we achieve
perfect accuracy within this scope, (b) our new dataset-size analysis provides
quantitative guidance for how much data would be needed to extend to new
authors, and (c) our embedding comparison provides an additional baseline.
Extending to larger author sets is an important direction for future work, and
our dataset-size analysis provides the foundation for estimating the
computational resources required.}

\textbf{R3:} Lack of demonstration of generalization ability across genres.

\response{Our Oz book analysis provides some evidence of within-author
cross-topic robustness. We have expanded our discussion of this point and
identified systematic cross-genre evaluation as an important direction for
future work.}
